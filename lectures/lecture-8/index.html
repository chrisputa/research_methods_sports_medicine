Spiroergometry — Method, Quality, V̇O₂max and Ventilatory Thresholds

Learning objectives:

  • Name the established spiroergometric indicators of endurance capacity and what each represents
  • Apply the methodological quality requirements of a gas-exchange measurement
  • Distinguish the ventilatory threshold from the respiratory compensation point
  • Interpret V̇O₂max against reference values in athletes and in patients

1 What Spiroergometry Measures

During spiroergometry, oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide output are measured continuously under load, usually under laboratory conditions with a ramp or step protocol. The established indicators of endurance capacity are maximal oxygen uptake (V̇O₂max) and the ventilatory threshold (VT); the respiratory compensation point (RCP) is used less often (Scharhag-Rosenberger 2010). V̇O₂max is the most familiar single value of endurance capacity: it represents the highest quantity of oxygen metabolised and therefore the joint function of all organ systems that exchange, transport and consume oxygen.

Reference expectations: up to about 90 ml·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ in highly trained endurance athletes; around 60 ml·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ on average in professional footballers; roughly 35–50 ml·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ in healthy untrained 30-year-old women and men. From the age of 30 V̇O₂max declines by about 8–10 % per decade. Because of their smaller muscle mass, women’s values lie roughly 10–15 % below men’s. In patients with severe heart failure, V̇O₂max lies approximately between 15 and 20 ml·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ (Scharhag 2021).

2 Methodological Quality

Good data quality is not a by-product of an expensive device. The practical requirements are concrete: depending on the model, devices need roughly 30 minutes of warm-up; calibration follows the manufacturer’s instructions and, where ambient air is used for it, the room is ventilated before calibration and measurement. The mask must fit tightly — with the opening closed, no air should escape on expiration. Test persons should not speak during the measurement. Ventilation, V̇O₂ and the respiratory quotient are checked for plausibility before the start and during the test. Consumables are checked regularly for wear and deposits and replaced. Even small measurement errors can be detected by biological quality control, in which a standardised load is repeated with the same test person at regular intervals after an initial test series (Scharhag-Rosenberger 2010).

Protocol duration matters for the validity of the maximal value: exhaustion tests up to a maximum of about 26 minutes appear suitable for measuring V̇O₂max. Threshold determination and maximal-value determination place partly conflicting demands on the protocol, so the primary question has to be stated before the protocol is chosen.

3 The Two Ventilatory Thresholds

As load increases, ventilation rises in a characteristically non-linear way, and two breakpoints can be identified. The first, the ventilatory threshold (VT1), marks the onset of a disproportionate rise in ventilation relative to oxygen uptake and corresponds in position to the aerobic lactate threshold. The second, the respiratory compensation point (RCP, VT2), lies in the region of the anaerobic lactate threshold without being point-for-point identical with it. The physiological interpretation of RCP is that in this intensity range the hydrogen ions arising from lactic acid can no longer be fully buffered, so that the falling pH additionally stimulates ventilation (Kindermann 2004).

Thresholds are not always unambiguously identifiable in an individual test, and determination carries an interpretive component. The honest methodological statement is this: a threshold is a model-based reading of a curve, and two competent readers can differ. VT is widely used in clinical settings and in prevention because it is submaximal and therefore does not require an exhausting test.

4 Derived Variables and the Nine-Panel Display

The nine-panel display is worked through in the laboratory demonstration accompanying this lecture; students bring one anonymised report each.

Beyond the maximal value, the relationships between the primary measures carry the diagnostic information: ventilatory equivalents for O₂ and CO₂, the respiratory quotient, the O₂ pulse as a surrogate of stroke volume, and the slope of V̇O₂ against power. In patients, spiroergometry frequently serves the non-invasive clarification of dyspnoea: by displaying individual measures and their relationships — conventionally as a nine-panel graphic — the functions of the cardiocirculatory system, of ventilation and of gas exchange can be separated, and conclusions drawn about the cause of the breathlessness. V̇O₂max is also used in patients with internal organ disease to estimate operative risk or survival probability, for example in severe heart failure or COPD (Scharhag 2021). This closes the loop to Lecture 4: the vocabulary of dyspnoea acquires a quantitative decision procedure here.

One-Minute-Paper Topics

A One-Minute-Paper (OMP) is a short, focused prompt that students answer in ~60 seconds at the end of a session to consolidate learning, surface misconceptions, and provide formative feedback. When answering, be concise, specific, and use terminology from today’s session.

  • List four methodological requirements of a valid gas-exchange measurement and state what each protects against.
  • Why is VT attractive in clinical and preventive settings?
  • Explain the physiological reasoning behind the respiratory compensation point.
  • A 30-year-old untrained woman has a V̇O₂max of 32 ml·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹. Place this value and name two non-pathological explanations.
  • Why can a protocol optimised for V̇O₂max be suboptimal for threshold determination?
