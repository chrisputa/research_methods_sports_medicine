Lactate Performance Diagnostics — Protocols, Thresholds, Zones

Table of Contents

Learning objectives:

  • Define the aerobic threshold and the individual anaerobic threshold and relate them to VT1 and VT2
  • Justify why fixed lactate thresholds are inadequate and when they may still be used
  • Construct a step protocol suitable for lactate diagnostics and name its error sources
  • Derive training zones from the individual anaerobic threshold and interpret curve shifts

1 Why Thresholds Rather Than Maxima

The classical laboratory principle of performance testing is spiroergometry with measurement of V̇O₂max. There is, however, a clear discrepancy between sport-specific performance development — for example in long-distance running, where performance is well measurable — and the increase in V̇O₂max. Already in the 1960s and 1970s, values around or above 80 ml·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ were being reported, and the same holds for heart volumes, whose maximal values approached 20 ml·kg⁻¹ three decades ago (Kindermann 2004). When maximal values converge at the top of a discipline, they stop discriminating, and the submaximal threshold takes over as the informative variable. The anaerobic threshold is additionally motivation-independent, which the maximal test is not.

2 The Two Thresholds

Aerobic threshold (AeS/AeT) — the point of the first systematic rise of lactate above baseline; it marks the beginning of the aerobic–anaerobic transition and is assigned to the first ventilatory threshold (VT1). Individual anaerobic threshold (IAS/IAT) — the upper boundary of the aerobic–anaerobic transition, representing the maximal lactate steady state: the highest workload at which production and elimination remain in equilibrium. It is assigned to VT2 / the respiratory compensation point, which lies in its region without being point-identical. Above the anaerobic threshold, lactate concentration rises during prolonged constant-intensity work despite unchanged intensity; this is what makes the threshold a genuine physiological breakpoint rather than a convention (Kindermann 2004).

Individual thresholds permit a more reliable assessment of endurance capacity and a more reliable steering of intensity than fixed lactate thresholds. The fixed 4 mmol·l⁻¹ value remains defensible only as a coarse group-level orientation, because the lactate concentration at the maximal steady state varies substantially with training status, muscle fibre composition, glycogen status and the protocol used. Method-based determinations — among them the approach of Stegmann et al. (1981), which incorporates lactate kinetics during recovery — make the individual determination explicit rather than assumed.

3 Protocol and Measurement

Röcker (2019) treats these as the substance of the method rather than as footnotes: the quality criteria of lactate diagnostics — validity, reliability, objectivity and practicability — are properties of the whole procedure, not of the analyser. Field step tests, for example a running protocol with 3-minute steps and stepwise increases of roughly 2 km·h⁻¹, are the practicable variant, with their specific limitations on blood sampling and on environmental standardisation.

4 Zones and Curve Shifts

Longitudinally, the shape of the curve carries the message. A rightward shift — the same lactate concentration now occurring at a higher workload — indicates improved mitochondrial capacity, enhanced monocarboxylate transporter expression and better fat oxidation, and is the signature of successful endurance adaptation. A leftward shift indicates that lactate accumulates at lower workloads and signals detraining, overreaching, acute infection or post-infectious deconditioning. Repeated testing with attention to test–retest variability is required before a shift is called; a single pair of curves within the measurement error is not a finding.

Clinical caveat. Patients with ME/CFS and Long COVID can show pathological leftward shifts at minimal effort. In these groups, zone-based prescription derived from a threshold test may be actively harmful, and the diagnostic question reverts to the one posed in Lecture 3: is exertion a tolerable stimulus at all?

One-Minute-Paper Topics

A One-Minute-Paper (OMP) is a short, focused prompt that students answer in ~60 seconds at the end of a session to consolidate learning, surface misconceptions, and provide formative feedback. When answering, be concise, specific, and use terminology from today’s session.

  • Why is a step duration of at least three minutes required for lactate diagnostics but not for ventilatory thresholds?
  • Define the maximal lactate steady state and explain why it is a breakpoint rather than a convention.
  • An athlete’s curve has shifted left by 15 W at 4 mmol·l⁻¹ since the last test. Name four possible explanations and the information you would need to decide between them.
  • Why does the anaerobic threshold outperform V̇O₂max as an endurance indicator in an elite cohort?
  • Give two reasons why a fixed 4 mmol·l⁻¹ threshold can mislead.
